What a CRM does for a local business

CRM stands for customer relationship management. For a plumber, salon, dental office or contractor, that means three practical jobs:

Know every customer

Keep a complete history of every call, text and email on one record, so you never ask a customer to repeat themselves.

Reply quickly

Manage all your conversations from one app and reply to texts, emails and Facebook messages without switching tools.

Track your revenue

See how many leads you have, what stage each one is in and how much potential revenue is in your pipeline.

Large, general-purpose CRMs are often more complicated than a local business needs, and spreadsheets get messy as soon as more than one person is answering the phone. A local business CRM sits in between: simple enough to use every day, complete enough to replace the patchwork of separate tools.

Why local businesses need a CRM

Local businesses need a CRM to organize customer data, capture leads efficiently, automate follow-up and keep potential sales from slipping away. The typical warning signs are familiar:

  • Calls go to voicemail while you are on a job, and some of those callers never call back.
  • Leads arrive by phone, text, web form, Facebook and Google, and nobody sees all of them in one place.
  • Quotes go out but nobody follows up, so you never learn whether the customer chose someone else.
  • You pay for a separate texting app, email tool, scheduler and review tool that do not share data.
  • You cannot say with confidence how much work is in the pipeline this month.

What to look for in a CRM for local businesses

  1. One inbox for every channel. Calls, SMS, email, Facebook Messenger and Instagram messages should land in one thread per customer. See omnichannel messaging.
  2. Missed-call text-back. When you cannot answer, the system should text the caller back automatically so the conversation keeps going. See missed-call text-back.
  3. A visual sales pipeline. Drag-and-drop stages that match how you actually sell, from inquiry to estimate to won job. See sales pipelines.
  4. Automated follow-up. Sequences of texts and emails that start when a form is submitted, a call is missed or a deal changes stage. See workflow automations.
  5. Built-in booking. Customers book directly into your calendar, with automated reminders. See appointment booking.
  6. Reviews and payments. Ask for reviews when a job is complete and collect payment by invoice or text. See reputation management and text-to-pay.
  7. Pricing that does not punish growth. Watch for per-user and per-contact fees that rise as you add staff or customers.
  8. A mobile app. Owners and field staff need to reply to leads from their phones. See the mobile app.
